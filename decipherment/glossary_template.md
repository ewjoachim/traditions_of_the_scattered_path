# Glossary and decipherment notes

The booklet is English underneath. Each English word has been swapped for an invented
one, and the verb has been moved to the front of its clause. With that, {{KNOWN}} of the
{{TOTAL}} words in the body text ({{PCT}}%) now have a proposed meaning, and most
sentences can be read at least in outline. A sentence-by-sentence draft is in
[translation.md](./translation.md).

Nothing here is confirmed by the author. Words are written in the arbitrary Latin letters
of the [transcript](./transcript/joined_text.txt), so the names below are labels, not
pronunciations.

The word list is kept in [decipherment/lexicon.tsv](./decipherment/lexicon.tsv). This page
and the translation are generated from the files in [decipherment/](./decipherment/), where
there are also two small tools for checking a word against every place it occurs.

Confidence:

- **A**: fixed by several independent contexts, usually including an English idiom or an agreement pattern.
- **B**: fits all or nearly all occurrences, with at least one context where little else fits.
- **C**: a guess from one to three contexts or from a picture. The word class is usually right even when the meaning is not.

The lists hold {{N_A}} words at A, {{N_B}} at B and {{N_C}} at C.

### 2. The verb comes first

None of the 206 sentences starts with `oe`, `leb`, `nene` or `ytoa`. The verb or its
helper is moved to the front, and a pronoun subject follows the first helper:

| In the booklet | Word for word | English |
|---|---|---|
| `rt oe wia fatpt` | is the whole broken | The whole is broken. |
| `ftcr ae nena …` | say some that | Some say that … |
| `medtk meuek ce ftcr nae wtuiyt` | can not we say for certain | We cannot say for certain. |
| `esa mtui nagtwe want utytse nrsi oe ade` | have been lost many pieces of the path | Many pieces of the path have been lost. |
| `esa hayr mtui nae westbrse` | have they been for centuries | They have been for centuries. |
| `nae wt hrdehi oe eltprw …` | for to practise the Eltprw | for the Eltprw to practise … |

`rt` goes with singular subjects and `dta` with plural ones (`rt nene …`, `rt leb …`,
`rt ide`; `dta ytoa …`, `dta hayr`, `dta oe drese`).

### 3. Endings

| Ending | English | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| `-se` | plural -s (also "whose", "one's") | `ade`/`adese`, `maaoi`/`maaoise`, `gehi`/`gehise` |
| `-oi` | possessive | `ide`/`ideoi` (it, its), `hayr`/`hayroi` (they, their), `ureoi` (potter's) |
| `-b` | -ed | `dige`/`digeb` (turn, turned), `ectle`/`ectleb` (exist, existed) |
| `-sa` | -s on verbs | `crltsa`, `uioiyisa`, `cttsa` |
| `-ye` | -er | `stsa`/`stsaye` (old, older), `sihr`/`sihrye` (long, longer), `crlr`/`crlrye` (large, larger) |
| `-we` | makes an adjective | `yaut`/`yautwe` (beauty, beautiful), `camtkur`/`camtkurwe` (continue, continuous) |

Irregular English forms have their own unrelated words: `rt`/`dta`/`ea`/`lefi`/`oi`/`mtui`
(is, are, was, were, be, been), `ftcr`/`laoab` (say, said), `lime`/`lrab` (make, made),
`hayr`/`twt` (they, them). A few families share a stem: `nifi`, `niftur`, `nifipt`
(know, known, knowledge); `dantft`, `dantftya` (writing, written).

### 5. Three short words with two unrelated meanings

| Word | Usual meaning | Other use |
|---|---|---|
| `wi` | may ("may be", "may have had") | true ("its true form", "if true") |
| `ta` | life ("a very private life", "beyond this life") | something like "said" ("not `ta` to have found…") |
| `rt` | is (57 of 58 uses) | once where "best" is expected: `gehi nrsi oe rt niftur eur mavmi nafilrb`, "one of the `rt` known but least understood" |

No English word covers both meanings in any of the three. All three are two-letter words,
so the simplest explanation is that the word generator handed the same short form to two
English words. The `rt` case is a single occurrence and could equally be a slip in the
author's source. It is printed that way in the booklet (page 15, checked against the scan).

## Open questions

- The cover word `drbwacr` never occurs in the body. The body's word for "tradition" is
  `fidesi`, so the cover may say something other than "Traditions".
- `megtlr` ("unseen") could instead be "natural" or "spirit": `oe megtlr ftca` is some
  kind of world, set against `oe yarhe ftca` and `oe cesana ftca`.
- `ur` / `uroi` is read as "she / her" because one word serves as object and possessive.
  If the author merged "him" and "his", it is "he", and `est` is "man", not "woman".
- `dtntkdt` ("bridges") and `hrdr` ("stair") both describe arch-and-stair structures and
  may be the other way round. `delact` ("structures") never takes the plural ending.
- `dayrwt` ("soul") rests on two phrases: `oe dayrwt hrdrse` and `uroi dayrwt`.
- The second meanings of `wi`, `ta` and `rt` (see above).
- "Scattered" has two words. `awrlr` (10 uses, including the cover title) never takes an
  ending; `ftaneb` (5 uses: "the old `ftaneb` path tradition", "our ideas, `ftaneb`, for
  each holds only a piece of the truth") ends in `-b` like a regular -ed form. One
  explanation is a recurring misspelling such as "scatered" in the author's English, which
  the word generator treated as a different word. A synonym ("shattered", "dispersed")
  cannot be ruled out.
- About half the distinct words occur once. Most of those remain unread, and they are
  where the bracketed guesses in the translation sit.
